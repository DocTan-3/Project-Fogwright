# The Wayfolk: culture sheet

Stage 0 of the city-planning skill for every Wayfolk settlement, and the common style of mixed places such as Farhallow
and Hollin's Cross. Choices made with the user are marked **(chosen)**; the rest are proposals to confirm or change.

The Wayfolk are the mixed peoples: travellers and commoners with no single specialty, the most numerous people in the
world, under **Hollin the Wayfarer**, god of the open road and the welcome at its end.

## How they live together

- **Household:** a family of parents, children and often a grandparent, plus apprentices and servants in better-off
  homes. They marry across every people, so faces, hair and names are mixed; a Wayfolk family tree is a map of the roads.
- **Power (chosen):** towns rule themselves. Householders elect a **reeve** each year, who keeps the peace, the
  waystone's ledger and the town's chest, and sits with a **council** of guild masters and merchants. Money buys a seat in
  practice, so the council is the rich, and the poor grumble about it at the inn. Villages have a reeve and a meeting of
  householders instead of a council.
- **What they value:** the road and the welcome at its end; a fair bargain; knowing everyone's news; being able to leave.
  A Wayfolk saying: "Every door is a waystone to someone."
- **What they hide:** that they belong nowhere. Other peoples call them rootless; Wayfolk call it free and don't like
  being reminded that no hometown is theirs alone.

## How their towns are arranged

- **The heart (chosen): the waystone and the inn.** Every Wayfolk town grows round a **waystone**, Hollin's shrine, at
  its main crossroads: a standing stone carved with the roads that leave it and the distances along them, with a lamp
  niche and an offering ledge. The town's **inn or common hall** faces it across a square; the market spills round both
  on trade days, and the reeve reads notices from the waystone's step. Arriving travellers meet the god and the welcome
  in the same place.
- **The waylodge (chosen):** next to the waystone, Hollin's priests keep a **free waylodge**, where any traveller may eat
  plain food and sleep one night at no cost. Its **road lamp** burns all night over the door. Paying guests go to the inn;
  the waylodge is for those who can't pay, and the priests hear the news of the roads there.
- **Streets:** organic, following the roads in. Main streets run from each gate to the waystone, widening into a market
  street near the centre; lanes and alleys run between them. No grid, no plan imposed from above.
- **Houses (chosen): face the street, shoulder to shoulder, on long narrow plots.** The front is a shop or workshop
  on the ground floor with the family living above; behind runs a long plot with a yard, outbuildings, a garden, the
  privy and a back gate onto a back lane. In villages the same plan spreads out with gaps between houses and bigger
  gardens.
- **Gates and walls:** towns have a wall and gates where the roads come in, each gate with a small waystone; villages
  have a fence or palisade. The gates stand open by day: closing them is shameful in Hollin's eyes.
- **The dead:** buried by the roadside outside the gates, each grave marked with a small stone carved with where the
  person was born, "so they know the way home".
- **Water and slope:** wells in the squares and big yards; mills on the rivers; they build on any ground, terracing
  slopes with stone retaining walls.

## How rich and poor show

| | Well-off (merchants, guild masters, the reeve) | Middling (craftsfolk, shopkeepers, farmers) | Poor (labourers, carters, fishers) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where | On the main street near the waystone, or uphill and upwind | Along the main streets and the lanes | Back lanes, by the gates, the docks and the smelly trades |
| House | Three storeys, wide front, stone ground floor, glazed windows, carved and painted beams | Two storeys, timber frame, a shop window with shutters | One storey and a loft, or rooms in a subdivided house |
| Roof | Clay tiles, dormers, tall chimneys | Tiles or wooden shingles | Thatch or shingles, patched |
| Plot | Walled garden, own well, stable, coach gate | Yard, workshop, small garden | Shared yard or none |
| Show | Flower boxes, brass door lantern, painted sign, a small boiler and pipes for hot water | Iron door lantern, a hanging sign | A clay lamp on a hook, a bench |

## Architecture

- **Construction:** timber frame on a stone plinth, with wattle-and-daub or brick infill, plastered and limewashed;
  stone ground floors for the well-off and on damp ground. **Jettied upper floors** overhang the street. Roofs are steep
  and gabled, end-on or side-on to the street, with clay tiles, shingles or thatch.
- **Colour and ornament:** painted beams (each family has its colour), flower boxes, carved door posts, and the **door
  lantern hook** every house has, even where no lantern hangs. Shop signs hang from iron brackets.
- **Light steampunk:** in towns near Aldrath, small boilers, copper pipes and gauges are added on to better houses and
  workshops; far out (Farhallow) there are almost none.
- **On slopes:** each house sits on its own terrace with a stone retaining wall; steps between terraces.

### Building types and floorplans

Drawn in `wayfolk-plans.png` (made by `tools/map/wayfolk_plans.py`). Sizes on the 2 m kit grid.

1. **Street house** (the common town house), 6 × 10 m, 2-3 storeys. Ground floor: shop or workshop at the front with the
   street door and shop window, a passage down one side to the yard, a kitchen with the hearth at the back. Upper
   floors: hall (living room) over the shop, jettied over the street; bedrooms behind. Long plot behind: yard,
   outbuilding, garden, privy, back gate.
2. **Cottage** (villages, the poor), 6 × 8 m, 1 storey and a loft. One main room with the hearth in the middle of the back
   wall, a sleeping alcove, a loft over half the room by ladder; a lean-to store at the back. The door faces the street or
   lane, with a bench beside it.
3. **Merchant's house**, 10 × 12 m, 3 storeys. Wide front with a central door, the counting room and a shop on either side,
   a coach gate to a yard with a stable and store; a hall and parlour upstairs, bedrooms above; a walled garden behind.
4. **Inn**, 12 × 16 m round a yard. Taproom with a big hearth and the bar on the street front, kitchen behind, a stable
   and coach yard through an archway, guest rooms on the floor above and in a gallery round the yard.
5. **Waylodge**, 8 × 12 m. A single long common room with benches, a hearth and a long table; sleeping lofts or
   bunks at one end; the priest's room and the store at the other. The road lamp over the door. It always shares a
   square with the waystone.
6. **Waystone shrine**: a standing stone about 2.5 m tall on a stepped base, carved with roads and distances, a lamp
   niche facing the main road, an offering ledge; in towns, under a small open roof on four posts.
7. **Workshop with yard** (smithy, cooper, carpenter), 8 × 10 m: an open-fronted work bay on the street with the forge or
   bench, a store behind, the yard to one side for fuel and materials. Noisy and smoky trades sit at the downwind end.
8. **Farmstead** (outside towns), a house like the cottage but longer, with a barn and byre across a yard, all facing
   the yard, and the yard gate facing the road.

## What a stranger notices first

1. A **carved waystone** at every crossroads, with a lamp burning in its niche, telling you how far everything is.
2. **Lanterns by every door** at dusk, and the road lamp of the waylodge.
3. **Jettied, painted timber houses** crowding a busy street, with every people's face in the crowd.

## For mixed places (Farhallow, Hollin's Cross)

- **Hollin's Cross** is Hollin's own town: fully Wayfolk, the greatest waystone of all at the crossroads by the harbour.
- **Farhallow** lies outside any god's circle, but its freefolk mostly build the Wayfolk way, poorer and plainer: cottages
  and village street houses, thatch and shingles, few tiles, almost no pipes. *(proposal)* An old waystone stands by the
  well, older than the village, its lamp kept lit by the shrine keeper; a few families of other peoples built in their
  own style, to be decided when those culture sheets are written.
